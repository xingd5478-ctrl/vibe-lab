The 43-lesson English guide

Every lesson in English: goal, concepts, formula, steps, what to watch, pitfalls and a self-check. Download the .slx model or the original .m; the Chinese source notes remain available for comparison.

After 43 lessons you should be able to: build first/second-order and closed-loop models, read Bode and step responses, explain the trade-offs in PID, state feedback, observers and Kalman filters, and know when a real calibration is still required.

Phase 1 · Simulink & dynamics t01–t08

t01 Signals

Think of Simulink as a water pipe: the signal is the water, Gain is a valve, Scope is the flow meter.

Goal. Build a sine → gain → scope model on your own and explain what each block does.

Concepts

  • Signal — A number that changes with time; in Simulink it is just a line.
  • Gain — Multiplies the input by a constant: K=2 means twice as large.
  • Scope — An oscilloscope; double-click it to see the waveform.
y(t) = K · u(t)

Steps

  1. Drag Sine Wave from Sources
  2. Drag Gain from Math Operations and change it to 2
  3. Drag Scope from Sinks
  4. Connect the lines in order and click Run
  5. Double-click Scope to see the waveform

Watch for. The sine amplitude in Scope becomes twice the input; set K to 0.5 and the amplitude halves at once.

Pitfall. Scope does not pop up by itself, you must double-click it; Sine defaults to 1 rad/s, not 1 Hz.

Check yourself

  1. What is the output amplitude when K=0.5?
    Half of the input.
  2. If Scope shows no waveform, what do you check first?
    The wiring, whether Run was clicked, and whether Scope was opened.

t02 Math blocks

Wiring in Simulink is like parcel sorting: Sum merges, Product multiplies, Mux packs.

Goal. Use Sum/Product/Mux to add, subtract and multiply several signals and display them together.

Concepts

  • Sum — Adds or subtracts several inputs; the +/− port signs can be changed.
  • Product — Multiplies several inputs.
  • Mux — Packs several signals into one bus so a single Scope can show multiple curves.
y = (u1 + u2) · K

Steps

  1. Drag two Sine blocks as u1 and u2
  2. Drag Sum and change the signs to + +
  3. Drag Gain and set K
  4. Drag Mux to bundle both inputs and the result
  5. Connect Scope and run

Watch for. Scope shows u1, u2 and their sum; change K and the sum curve scales as a whole.

Pitfall. Sum input ports default to + +, double-click to subtract; Mux only packs, it performs no math.

Check yourself

  1. Does Mux add the signals together?
    No, it only packs them for display.
  2. How do you make Sum compute u1−u2?
    Double-click Sum and change the second port sign to −.

t03 First order

A first-order system is like a water tank: the inlet is open, but the level creeps toward the top instead of jumping.

Goal. Understand the time constant τ: larger τ is slower; read response speed with the 63.2% and 2% rules.

Concepts

  • Time constant τ — Time needed for the level to reach 63.2% of its final value.
  • Steady-state gain K — How many times the input the final level is.
  • Settling time — About 4τ until the response enters the ±2% band.
G(s) = K / (τs + 1); step response y(t) = K(1 − e^(−t/τ))

Steps

  1. Drag Step as the input
  2. Drag Transfer Fcn with numerator [K] and denominator [τ 1]
  3. Connect Scope and Run
  4. Change τ to see the speed, change K to see the final height

Watch for. With τ=1 the response reaches 63.2% at 1 s; with τ=3 it reaches the same height but needs 3 s. K changes only the final height, not the speed.

Pitfall. Transfer Fcn denominator is [τ 1], not [1 τ]; Step jumps at 1 s by default, not at 0 s.

Check yourself

  1. With τ=2 s, when is 63.2% reached?
    At about 2 s (counted from the step instant).
  2. Does a larger K make the response faster?
    No, it changes only the final amplitude.

t04 Second order

A second-order system is like a car on springs: it has inertia and damping, so it overshoots and then rebounds.

Goal. Explain what ωn and ζ each control; read overshoot and oscillation from the waveform.

Concepts

  • ωn natural frequency — How fast the system wants to oscillate; larger is faster.
  • ζ damping ratio — How hard the brakes are; ζ<1 overshoots, ζ=1 is critical, ζ>1 is overdamped.
  • Overshoot — The largest percentage by which the first peak exceeds the target.
G(s)=ωn²/(s²+2ζωn s+ωn²); overshoot ≈ exp(−πζ/√(1−ζ²))

Steps

  1. Step input
  2. Transfer Fcn with denominator [1 2ζωn ωn²] and numerator [ωn²]
  3. Connect Scope and run
  4. Change ωn and ζ separately and compare

Watch for. Raising ζ from 0.2 to 0.7 clearly reduces the overshoot; a larger ωn speeds up the whole curve, but ζ sets the shape.

Pitfall. More damping is not always better: too large a ζ slows the response; look at ωn and ζ together.

Check yourself

  1. Does ζ=1 overshoot?
    No, that is critical damping.
  2. To go faster without overshoot, what do you tune?
    Keep ζ≈0.7 first, then increase ωn.

t05 PID control

PID is like driving: P steps on the gas for the current error, I remembers the lasting error, D lifts off early based on the rate of change.

Goal. Understand which problem P/I/D each solve; read overshoot, settling time and steady-state error.

Concepts

  • P — The larger the error, the harder the control; P alone usually leaves steady-state error.
  • I — Accumulates the error and removes steady-state error; too much causes overshoot or windup.
  • D — Reacts to the error rate and suppresses overshoot; it amplifies noise.
u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt + Kd·de/dt

Steps

  1. Step → Sum (reference − output)
  2. Feed the Sum into PID Controller
  3. Connect the PID output to the plant (e.g. 1/(s²+2ζωn s+ωn²))
  4. Feedback the output to the Sum and connect Scope

Watch for. P only: steady-state error remains; add I: the error disappears but overshoot may appear; add D: less overshoot but more noise.

Pitfall. I can wind up; D is noise-sensitive, so filter it in practice. Tune P first, then I, then D.

Check yourself

  1. Which term removes steady-state error?
    I.
  2. With large overshoot and noise, what do you adjust first?
    Reduce Kd or filter D, then retune Kp.

t06 Sources & sinks

Simulink is not an island: data can come in from the MATLAB workspace and results can be saved back.

Goal. Import data with From Workspace and export simulation results with To Workspace.

Concepts

  • From Workspace — Reads a time series from a MATLAB variable.
  • To Workspace — Saves the simulation output as a variable for analysis at the command line afterwards.
  • Sample time — The time column of the data must match the model sample time.
Variable format: t = (0:0.01:10)'; u = sin(t);

Steps

  1. Prepare t and u at the command line
  2. Drag From Workspace and enter [t u]
  3. Connect Scope or Gain
  4. Drag To Workspace to save y
  5. After Run, plot(tout, y) at the command line

Watch for. The Scope waveform should match plot(t,u); the To Workspace variable appears after Run.

Pitfall. A misspelled variable name, a non-monotonic time column and a missing sample time all raise errors or give a bad waveform.

Check yourself

  1. How does data enter Simulink?
    Through From Workspace.
  2. How do results get back to MATLAB?
    Through To Workspace.

t07 Subsystems

A subsystem puts a tangle of wires in a box: outside you see only inputs and outputs, inside you tidy up as you like.

Goal. Wrap several blocks into a subsystem and understand hierarchical modeling.

Concepts

  • Subsystem — A container for a group of blocks that exposes only input/output ports.
  • Port order — The subsystem input/output order decides the external wiring.
  • Reuse — The same subsystem can be called in many places.
Outside: u → [subsystem] → y; inside it is still the original block wiring.

Steps

  1. Select the blocks to wrap
  2. Right-click Create Subsystem from Selection
  3. Double-click to enter the subsystem and check the ports
  4. Name the subsystem, wire it outside and run

Watch for. The waveform is identical before and after wrapping; you can still double-click inside to change parameters.

Pitfall. Port order may change after wrapping; changes to internal parameters are not visible from outside.

Check yourself

  1. Does a subsystem change the system dynamics?
    No, it is only an organizational structure.
  2. How do you enter a subsystem?
    Double-click the subsystem block.

t08 Masks

A Mask is a dashboard for a subsystem: internal parameters become a dialog so others do not have to go inside.

Goal. Add a Mask to a subsystem and build a reusable, configurable block.

Concepts

  • Mask — A custom parameter panel for a subsystem.
  • Parameters — K, τ and so on in the panel, mapped to internal blocks.
  • Initialization — A Mask can run initialization code to compute initial values from the parameters.
Panel parameter K → internal Gain value = K.

Steps

  1. Right-click the subsystem → Mask → Create Mask
  2. Add a parameter named K under Parameters
  3. Enter K as the gain of the internal Gain block
  4. Confirm, then double-click the subsystem to see the panel

Watch for. Double-clicking the subsystem shows only the parameter panel; after changing K the internal Gain follows and the waveform changes.

Pitfall. The parameter name must match the internal reference; click Apply or the change has no effect.

Check yourself

  1. What does a Mask do?
    Exposes internal parameters as a dialog for easy reuse.
  2. If a panel parameter has no effect, what do you check first?
    Whether the parameter name matches the internal reference.

Phase 2 · Classical control t16, t17, t22

t16 Lesson 16

A Bode plot is a taste test for the system: it follows low frequencies, lags at high frequencies, and may resonate in between.

Goal. Read the magnitude/phase plots, the −3 dB bandwidth, the resonant peak and the phase margin.

Concepts

  • Magnitude — How much each input frequency is amplified or attenuated, in dB.
  • Phase — How many degrees the output lags the input.
  • Margin — How much room is left before instability; phase margin is usually 45°–60°.
|G(jω)|dB = 20·log10|G(jω)|; a second-order system resonates near ω≈ωn√(1−2ζ²)

Steps

  1. Build a second-order transfer function
  2. Connect a Bode block or call bode(sys)
  3. Read the low-frequency gain, the −3 dB point and the resonant peak
  4. Change ζ and watch the peak and the phase

Watch for. Small ζ → tall resonant peak and steep phase change; large ζ → the peak disappears, bandwidth widens but the response slows.

Pitfall. dB is 20log10, not 10log10; the horizontal axis is logarithmic, so equal spacing is not equal frequency.

Check yourself

  1. The −3 dB point corresponds to about what magnitude?
    0.707 times.
  2. What happens if the phase margin is too small?
    Easy oscillation/instability.

t17 Lesson 17

Lead compensation is steering early; lag compensation is waiting a little and wearing the error down slowly.

Goal. Understand how zeros and poles change phase and gain; know that Lead raises phase margin and Lag raises low-frequency gain.

Concepts

  • Lead — The zero is left of the pole, adding phase in the mid band and improving speed/margin.
  • Lag — The pole sits near the origin, raising low-frequency gain and reducing steady-state error.
  • Frequency shaping — You do not add zeros and poles at random, you shape the target band.
Lead: Gc=(1+αTs)/(1+Ts), α>1; Lag: α<1

Steps

  1. Look at the uncompensated Bode plot, find the crossover frequency and phase margin
  2. Add a Lead zero/pole and watch the phase lift
  3. Add Lag when steady-state accuracy is needed
  4. Verify overshoot and settling time with a closed-loop step

Watch for. Lead increases the phase margin and reduces overshoot; Lag raises low-frequency gain and cuts steady-state error, but slows the response.

Pitfall. Lead amplifies high-frequency noise; Lag reduces bandwidth. Zeros and poles cannot be placed blindly, always look at the band.

Check yourself

  1. Which one raises the phase margin?
    Lead.
  2. Which one reduces steady-state error?
    Lag.

t22 Root locus

The root locus is the route the closed-loop poles take across the map as K grows from 0.

Goal. Read a root locus: when the system is stable, when it oscillates, and roughly what the critical K is.

Concepts

  • Open-loop gain K — Changing K moves the closed-loop poles along the locus.
  • Branches — One route starting from each open-loop pole.
  • Imaginary axis — The locus crossing it means stability changes.
Closed-loop characteristic equation 1 + K·G(s) = 0

Steps

  1. Write the open-loop G(s)
  2. Plot with rlocus(G) (X=poles, O=zeros)
  3. Find the imaginary-axis crossing (critical K)
  4. Pick the K on the locus that gives the desired damping

Watch for. Small K keeps the poles near the open-loop poles; as K grows some poles move into the right half-plane and the system goes from stable to oscillatory/unstable.

Pitfall. The root locus shows pole positions only, it does not prove enough margin; too large a K is unstable; zeros matter too.

Check yourself

  1. What does a locus crossing the imaginary axis mean?
    Critical stability.
  2. Does a larger K always mean faster?
    No, it may oscillate or even destabilize.

Phase 3 · Modern control t09–t12

t09 State space

State is the minimum information needed to remember where the system is now: to drive you only need position + velocity, not the whole history.

Goal. Write a second-order system as ẋ=Ax+Bu, y=Cx+Du and explain what A/B/C/D each do.

Concepts

  • State vector — A set of variables that determines the future evolution, such as [position, velocity].
  • A / B — A describes how the system evolves by itself, B describes how the input enters.
  • C / D — C picks which combination of states you measure, D is input feedthrough (usually 0).
Double integrator: ẋ1=x2, ẋ2=u; A=[0 1;0 0], B=[0;1], C=[1 0], D=0

Steps

  1. Drag a State-Space block
  2. Fill the A, B, C, D matrices (semicolons separate rows)
  3. Step → State-Space → Scope
  4. Run; then change C to [0 1] and look again

Watch for. C=[1 0] outputs position; C=[0 1] outputs only velocity, which becomes a constant — you are only 'measuring' velocity.

Pitfall. MATLAB matrices use semicolons for rows and spaces/commas for columns; leaving D out raises a dimension error; initial values live in the block's Initial condition.

Check yourself

  1. What does C do?
    Decides which combination of states is the output.
  2. What are the states of a double-integrator system?
    Position and velocity.

t10 State feedback / LQR

Pole placement is choosing the system's personality: fast, stable or overshoot-free all show up as pole locations.

Goal. Understand how u=−Kx moves the closed-loop poles to chosen positions; know what LQR's Q/R trade off.

Concepts

  • State feedback K — Multiply each state by a gain and sum them to form the control input.
  • Closed-loop poles — Set the response speed and oscillation; the further left, the faster.
  • LQR Q/R — Large Q cares more about state error (more aggressive), large R cares more about saving control effort (more conservative).
Double integrator: K=[ωn², 2ζωn], u=k1(r−x1)−k2x2, closed-loop poles −ζωn ± jωn√(1−ζ²)

Steps

  1. Step → Sum (+ −)
  2. Feed the Sum into a Gain as the control u
  3. Feed u into 1/s² (two Integrators in series)
  4. Multiply x1, x2 by K and feed them back to Sum
  5. Connect Scope to see position

Watch for. Raising ζ from 0.3 to 1 removes the overshoot; a larger ωn speeds up the response but also raises the control peak.

Pitfall. K is only feedback gain, the reference needs separate feedforward (otherwise the steady state never reaches 1); the control saturates, so limit it in practice.

Check yourself

  1. What do poles further left mean?
    Faster convergence.
  2. Does a larger R in LQR make control more aggressive or more conservative?
    More conservative.

t11 Observer

An observer is like an experienced driver: without opening the engine, engine speed can be guessed from the sound and the road speed.

Goal. Understand how a Luenberger observer corrects its state estimate with the output error, and that L sets the convergence speed.

Concepts

  • Estimate x̂ — Predict with the model, correct with the measurement y.
  • Observer gain L — Larger L trusts the measurement more and converges faster, but amplifies noise.
  • Separation principle — Controller and observer can be designed separately without interfering.
x̂' = Ax̂ + Bu + L(y − Cx̂); error e=x−x̂ converges as (A−LC)

Steps

  1. Copy the plant model as the observer
  2. Feed u into both the plant and the observer
  3. Subtract ŷ from y, multiply by L and add it back to the observer
  4. Use the observer states for feedback; Scope compares true vs estimated

Watch for. Deliberately start with a wrong estimate: the error decays at a rate set by L; too large an L makes noise shake the estimate.

Pitfall. Observer poles are usually 2–5 times faster than the controller's; when only position is measured, velocity is computed and its noise is amplified.

Check yourself

  1. What corrects the observer estimate?
    The output error y−Cx̂.
  2. Is a larger L always better?
    No, it amplifies noise and may destabilize.

t12 Kalman filter

A Kalman filter is a weighted average in noise: trust the model more if it is reliable, trust the measurement more if it is reliable.

Goal. Understand the predict/update steps and what larger Q and R each mean.

Concepts

  • Q process noise — How unreliable the model is; large Q → trust the measurement more.
  • R measurement noise — How noisy the sensor is; large R → trust the model more.
  • Kalman gain K — The measurement weight computed automatically at each step.
Predict P←P+Q; update K=P/(P+R), x̂←x̂+K(z−x̂), P←(1−K)P

Steps

  1. Generate a noisy measurement z
  2. Initialize x̂ and P
  3. Predict, then add Q, at each step
  4. Compute K, correct with z, update P
  5. Plot z against x̂

Watch for. Larger R gives a smoother estimate that lags real changes; larger Q follows the measurement more closely but is noisier.

Pitfall. Swapping Q/R is the most common mistake: R is sensor noise, not a 'trust level'; keep the units consistent.

Check yourself

  1. With larger R, what does the filter trust more?
    The model.
  2. What does a large K mean?
    This step trusts the measurement more.

Phase 4 · Advanced control t18–t21, t23, t24

t18 System ID

System identification is tasting the soup to guess the recipe: apply a known input and infer the model parameters from the output.

Goal. Estimate first-order K and τ from a step response; know least squares and model validation.

Concepts

  • Step-response method — Read K and τ directly from the final value and the 63.2% time.
  • Least squares — Fit parameters to data so the sum of squared prediction errors is minimal.
  • Model validation — Hold back a data segment and check whether predictions still hold.
First order: y(t)=K(1−e^(−t/τ)); K=y(∞), τ=t₆₃

Steps

  1. Apply a step input to the plant and log the output
  2. Read the final value K and the 63.2% time τ
  3. Refine with least squares
  4. Validate on another data segment

Watch for. The fitted curve should hug the measurements; a large deviation on new data means the model structure is wrong.

Pitfall. Too small an excitation gives poor SNR; remove offset/delay first; never validate on the fitting data itself.

Check yourself

  1. How do you read τ for a first-order system?
    The time to reach 63.2% of the response.
  2. Why keep a validation set?
    To prevent overfitting and test generalization.

t19 H-infinity

H∞ is insurance for the system: keep stability margin where the model is uncertain.

Goal. Understand the sensitivity function S, the complementary sensitivity T, and what the weighting functions suppress.

Concepts

  • S sensitivity — Effect of disturbance/model error on the output; it should be small at low frequency.
  • T complementary sensitivity — Effect of noise/high-frequency uncertainty; it should be small at high frequency.
  • Weights — W1/W2/W3 tell the optimizer which frequency band matters more.
min ‖[W1S; W2KS; W3T]‖∞ < 1

Steps

  1. Build the nominal model and the uncertainty
  2. Choose the sensitivity weight W1 and the control weight W2
  3. Solve for the controller with hinfsyn
  4. μ analysis / time-domain validation

Watch for. With weights, S is pushed down at low frequency and T at high frequency; the robust stability margin grows.

Pitfall. Harsher weights are not better: they may be infeasible or blow up the controller order; the nominal model must stay realistic.

Check yourself

  1. What does a large S mean?
    Disturbance/error strongly affects the output.
  2. What does H∞ optimize?
    The worst-case gain (∞ norm).

t20 MPC

MPC is like chess: look N moves ahead each time, play one move, then recompute N moves.

Goal. Understand the prediction model, receding-horizon optimization and constraints, and what Q/R trade off.

Concepts

  • Prediction model — Use the model to roll N steps forward and predict future states.
  • Receding horizon — Apply only the first move, then re-optimize at the next step.
  • Constraints — Input/state limits go straight into the optimization — MPC's killer feature.
min Σ [Q(x−r)² + R u²], s.t. |u|≤umax

Steps

  1. Discretize the plant model
  2. Set the prediction horizon N and the weights Q/R
  3. Add input/state constraints
  4. Solve the QP each step and apply the first u

Watch for. With a small uMax the input sits on the limit and the response slows; too small an N is shortsighted, a large N costs computation.

Pitfall. An inaccurate prediction model walks confidently in the wrong direction; conflicting constraints make the QP infeasible; check QP timing for real-time use.

Check yourself

  1. How many control moves does MPC apply per step?
    Only the first one.
  2. Is a larger N always better?
    Not necessarily, computation and model error both grow.

t21 Sliding mode

Sliding mode control presses the state onto a track: first reach the track, then slide along it to the target.

Goal. Understand the sliding surface s=ė+λe, the switching control u=−k·sat(s/φ), and chattering vs boundary layer.

Concepts

  • Sliding surface — s=0 is the constraint you want the state to obey.
  • Reaching condition — The control must guarantee that s converges to 0.
  • Boundary layer φ — Use sat instead of sign to reduce chattering at some accuracy cost.
s=ė+λe; u=−k·sat(s/φ)

Steps

  1. Define the error e and the sliding surface s
  2. Design the switching control so that s→0
  3. Suppress chattering with a boundary layer/super-twisting
  4. Look at the control signal and the error

Watch for. Small φ: small error but chattering control; large φ: smooth control but larger steady-state error; λ sets the convergence speed on the surface.

Pitfall. Chattering wears out actuators; too large a λ is noise-sensitive; k must exceed the disturbance bound.

Check yourself

  1. What happens when φ shrinks?
    More chattering, higher accuracy.
  2. How does the error converge on the surface?
    Exponentially, as ė=−λe.

t23 MRAC

MRAC adjusts the steering while driving: the reference model says how it should behave and the adaptation law changes its own parameters.

Goal. Understand the roles of the reference model, the tracking error and the adaptation law.

Concepts

  • Reference model — The ideal closed-loop response used as the learning target.
  • Tracking error — Difference between the real output and the reference-model output.
  • Adaptation law — Adjusts controller parameters online from the error (e.g. the MIT rule).
θ̇ = −γ·e·x (MIT rule, γ is the adaptation gain)

Steps

  1. Choose a reference model
  2. Define the tracking error
  3. Design an adaptation law to update the parameters
  4. Verify recovery after a parameter jump

Watch for. Wrong initial parameters give a large error that then shrinks as the parameters converge; too large a γ causes high-frequency oscillation.

Pitfall. Excessive adaptation gain destabilizes; insufficient excitation lets the parameters drift; add parameter limits.

Check yourself

  1. What is the reference model for?
    It defines the ideal response.
  2. Does a larger γ converge faster?
    Not necessarily, too large oscillates.

t24 Fuzzy logic

Fuzzy control writes the veteran's rule 'big error, push harder' as a rule table.

Goal. Understand membership, rules and defuzzification; know that it does not need an accurate model.

Concepts

  • Membership — The degree 0–1 to which a value belongs to big/medium/small.
  • Rule table — E.g. 'error positive big & error rate positive big → output positive big'.
  • Defuzzification — Weighted-averages the rule outputs into a single concrete control value.
u = Σ wᵢ·uᵢ / Σ wᵢ (Sugeno weighted average)

Steps

  1. Define the input membership functions
  2. Write the rule table
  3. Choose the defuzzification method
  4. Tune the scaling factors in closed loop

Watch for. Reasonable rules/scaling give a smooth response; too large overshoots, too small is sluggish; conflicting rules chatter.

Pitfall. More rules are not always better; unnormalized input scaling is hard to tune; fuzzy control has no stability guarantee.

Check yourself

  1. Does fuzzy control need an accurate model?
    No, it relies on rules/experience.
  2. What does defuzzification do?
    Combines the rule outputs into one concrete control value.

Phase 5 · Mechatronics & deployment t13–t15, t25

t13 DC motor

A motor has two layers of inertia: current takes time to build and speed takes time to build — hence the usual current inner loop + speed outer loop.

Goal. Understand back-EMF, the electrical and mechanical time constants, and why PI loops are cascaded.

Concepts

  • Back-EMF — The faster it spins, the larger the voltage the motor pushes back, which limits current.
  • Electrical τe=L/R — Current-loop response speed, usually milliseconds.
  • Mechanical τm=J/B — Speed-loop response speed, usually tens to hundreds of milliseconds.
Electrical: L di/dt = u − R i − Keω; mechanical: J dω/dt = Kt i − Bω

Steps

  1. First close only the current PI loop with a current step reference
  2. Then add the speed loop, whose output is the current reference
  3. Set the speed-loop bandwidth to 1/5–1/10 of the current loop
  4. Look at the speed and current waveforms

Watch for. The inner loop is fast and the outer loop is slower; a load-torque step drops the speed, which the outer loop pulls back.

Pitfall. Loops with too similar bandwidths oscillate; limiting and anti-windup are mandatory.

Check yourself

  1. Which loop should be faster?
    The current inner loop.
  2. What does back-EMF grow with?
    Speed.

t14 PMSM + FOC

FOC rotates the three-phase AC into a frame that turns with the rotor, so AC quantities become DC and control feels like a DC motor.

Goal. Understand the Clarke/Park transforms, the d-q axes, and why id is usually commanded to 0.

Concepts

  • Clarke — Three-phase abc → two-phase αβ.
  • Park — αβ → dq rotating with the rotor.

  3. d/q current PI

t15 Code generation

Code generation turns the diagram you built in Simulink into C code that the board can run.

Goal. Understand continuous→discrete (c2d), sample time, and what to check before generating code.

Concepts

  • Discretization c2d — Convert continuous A/B into discrete Ad/Bd stepped at the sample period.

x[k+1] = Ad x[k] + Bd u[k]; Ad=e^{ATs}

Pitfall. Inconsistent sample times make the code behave differently from the simulation; forgetting limits/anti-windup blows up the integrator.

Check yourself

  1. What does c2d do?
    Converts a continuous model into a discrete one.
  2. Is a larger Ts better?
    No, too large loses performance and can even destabilize.

t26 Robot kinematics

A robot arm is like your own arm: joint angles give the hand position (forward kinematics), and a target point can be converted back to joint angles (inverse kinematics).

Goal. Understand DH parameters, forward/inverse kinematics, multiple solutions and the reachable workspace.

Concepts

  • Forward kinematics FK — θ1, θ2 → end-effector position, by direct substitution.
  • Inverse kinematics IK — End-effector position → θ1, θ2, possibly with several solutions.
  • Singular/unreachable — A target too far away cannot be reached; some poses make joint speeds blow up.
x=l1cosθ1+l2cos(θ1+θ2); y=l1sinθ1+l2sin(θ1+θ2)

Steps

  1. Measure the link lengths l1, l2
  2. Write the forward kinematics equations
  3. Solve IK geometrically/analytically
  4. Check multiple solutions and reachability

Watch for. Joint angles change sharply near the boundary; one end point often has two elbow-up/elbow-down solutions.

Pitfall. Forgetting to pick among multiple solutions; targets beyond l1+l2 have no solution; unify the angle units and zero positions.

Check yourself

  1. What are the FK input and output?
    Joint angles → end-effector position.
  2. What happens if the target is too far?
    It is unreachable, no solution.

t27 MSD modeling

Mass-spring-damper is the classic wobbly system: give it a push and watch how it wobbles and when it stops.

Goal. Understand ωn and ζ, and that the TF/state-space/discrete/numerical-integration models are equivalent.

Concepts

  • ωn=√(k/m) — Natural vibration frequency without damping.
  • ζ=c/(2√(km)) — Damping ratio: <1 oscillates, =1 critical, >1 overdamped.
  • Four model forms — Transfer function, state space, discretization and numerical integration describe the same system.
m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = F(t); ωn=√(k/m), ζ=c/(2√(km))

Steps

  1. Set m, k, c
  2. Write the transfer function or state space
  3. Apply a step/impulse input
  4. Look at displacement, velocity and overshoot

Watch for. Larger k → higher frequency; larger c → less overshoot and faster settling; larger m → slower.

Pitfall. Inconsistent units (mm vs m); missing initial conditions; too large a discretization step causes numerical instability.

Check yourself

  1. Does ζ=0.2 oscillate?
    Yes, it is underdamped.
  2. How does ωn change as k grows?
    It grows.

t28 DSP basics

Sampling is like the frame rate of a movie: too few frames and the wheels seem to spin backwards — that is aliasing.

Goal. Understand the sampling theorem, aliasing, and the basic use of FFT/windows.

Concepts

  • Sampling theorem — fs > 2·fmax is required for distortion-free reconstruction.
  • Aliasing — Frequencies above fs/2 fold down into fake low frequencies.
  • FFT/window — Converts time to frequency; truncation leaks, so apply a window.
Nyquist frequency fs/2; aliased frequency = |f − round(f/fs)·fs|

Steps

  1. Feed in a sine
  2. Sample it at different fs
  3. Plot the samples/reconstructed waveform
  4. Run an FFT and check the spectral lines

Watch for. With enough fs the samples reconstruct the sine; with too little fs a fake low-frequency wave appears whose frequency is completely different from the original.

Pitfall. Time-domain 'looks fine' does not prove there is no aliasing; use an anti-alias filter before sampling; FFT resolution = fs/N.

Check yourself

  1. What is the highest frequency measurable with fs=40 Hz?
    20 Hz.
  2. What does a 30 Hz signal become when sampled at 40 Hz?
    A fake 10 Hz signal.

t29 MSD control compare

The same spring door can be closed by PID or by LQR, but one is tuned by trial and the other by model and cost.

Goal. Understand the difference between PID and LQR: one tunes on the error, the other optimizes over states.

Concepts

  • PID — No model needed, tune Kp/Ki/Kd; sensitive to noise/saturation.
  • LQR — Needs a state-space model, tune Q/R; better multivariable coordination.
  • Comparison axes — Overshoot, settling time, control energy, noise robustness.
PID: u=Kp e+Ki∫e+Kd de; LQR: u=−Kx, K from the Riccati equation with Q/R

Steps

  1. First tune PID to an acceptable response
  2. Build the state-space model and design LQR
  3. Add sensor noise/actuator limits
  4. Compare on the same plot

Watch for. A well-tuned LQR is usually smoother and uses less control energy; PID chatters more under noise.

Pitfall. Compare with the same limits and noise; a different LQR weight is a different controller, do not report only the best set.

Check yourself

  1. What does LQR need?
    A state-space model and Q/R.
  2. What is PID's advantage?
    No accurate model needed, easy to start with.

Phase 7 · Nonlinear filtering t31–t35

t31 EKF basics

The EKF walks a curve blindfolded: guess with the model at each step, correct with the sensor, but the model is nonlinear.

Goal. Understand the EKF predict/update and that the Jacobian performs local linearization.

Concepts

  • Nonlinear model — f(x) and h(x) are no longer matrix multiplications.
  • Jacobian — Differentiate f/h around the current state to get a local linear model.
  • Predict/update — Same as the KF, except the matrices are recomputed every step.
F=∂f/∂x, H=∂h/∂x; P⁻=F P Fᵀ+Q, K=P⁻Hᵀ(H P⁻Hᵀ+R)⁻¹

Steps

  1. Write the nonlinear state/measurement equations
  2. Derive the Jacobians F and H
  3. Initialize x̂ and P
  4. Predict/update at each step

Watch for. With a poor initial value it converges first and then tracks; under strong nonlinearity the linearization error makes the covariance over-optimistic.

Pitfall. A wildly wrong initial value or a wrong Jacobian diverges; Q/R swapped; angles must handle ±π wrapping.

Check yourself

  1. What is the main difference between EKF and KF?
    A Jacobian-based local linearization at every step.
  2. Does the EKF always converge?
    No, strong nonlinearity or bad initial values can diverge.

t32 EKF pendulum

The inverted pendulum is the bicycle of control: sinθ is strongly nonlinear and a slightly wrong initial state tips it over.

Goal. Understand the pendulum state equations, EKF estimation of angle/rate, and convergence from an initial offset.

Concepts

  • States — θ, θ̇ (sometimes cart position/velocity too).
  • Strong nonlinearity — sinθ and cosθ cannot be treated linearly.
  • Estimation — When only the angle is measured, the rate comes from the model/filter.
θ̈ = (g sinθ − u cosθ)/(l) (simplified); measurement h=θ

Steps

  1. Write the nonlinear pendulum equation
  2. Linearize or use an EKF
  3. Design the controller (LQR/energy)

t33 UKF

The UKF derives no derivatives; it scatters a few sigma points through the nonlinear function and refits the mean and covariance.

Goal. Understand the unscented transform: why you can filter nonlinear systems without Jacobians.

Concepts

  • Sigma points — 2n+1 representative points chosen from the covariance.
  • Unscented transform — Push the points through the nonlinearity, then weight them back into mean/covariance.
  • Weights — Center and symmetric points get different weights and can carry higher-order information.
xᵢ = x̂ ± √((n+λ)P); y = Σ wᵢ f(xᵢ)

Steps

  1. Choose the sigma points and weights
  2. Push each point through the nonlinear model
  3. Weight them into the predicted mean/covariance
  4. Update with the measurement

Watch for. Under strong nonlinearity the UKF is usually more accurate than the EKF; the cost grows with the dimension.

Pitfall. A non-positive-definite covariance or a bad weight choice diverges; the UKF is not magic — a wrong model breaks both.

Check yourself

  1. Does the UKF need Jacobians?
    No.
  2. How many sigma points are there?
    2n+1 (n is the state dimension).

t34 EKF vs UKF

EKF and UKF are two ways to guess a nonlinear distribution: one by tangent approximation, one by sampling.

Goal. Choose EKF or UKF based on nonlinearity strength, dimension and computation.

Concepts

  • EKF — First-order linearization, fast; large error under strong nonlinearity.
  • UKF — Sampling approximation, usually more accurate; cost about 2n+1 times.
  • Selection — Depends on nonlinearity, dimension and real-time requirements.
EKF: F=∂f/∂x; UKF: sigma-point propagation

Steps

  1. Implement EKF/UKF on the same model
  2. Run Monte Carlo over many initial values/noise seeds
  3. Compare RMSE, runtime and divergence rate
  4. Choose per scenario

Watch for. Under strong nonlinearity/large initial error the UKF has smaller error; on linear systems the two nearly coincide.

Pitfall. Concluding from a single run; ignoring computation; parameters not aligned (different Q/R).

Check yourself

  1. Does the UKF help much on linear systems?
    Not much, it is close to the KF.
  2. What is the UKF's cost?
    Higher computation.

t35 IMU fusion

A gyro is good short-term and an accelerometer is good long-term: a complementary filter trusts the gyro short-term and the accelerometer long-term.

Goal. Understand the complementary filter's α and its relation to the Kalman filter.

Concepts

  • Gyro integration — Smooth short-term, but it drifts.
  • Accelerometer — No long-term drift, but vibration/motion acceleration disturbs it.
  • Complementary α — Larger α trusts the gyro, smaller α trusts the accelerometer.
θ̂ ← α(θ̂ + ω·dt) + (1−α)·θacc

Steps

  1. Read the gyro rate and integrate it
  2. Read the accelerometer and compute the tilt
  3. Fuse them with weight α
  4. Tune α and watch drift/jitter

Watch for. Large α: smooth but slowly drifting; small α: responsive but jittery. A Kalman filter can be seen as an adaptive α.

Pitfall. During vigorous motion the accelerometer does not see pure gravity and biases the estimate; calibrate the gyro bias first.

Check yourself

  1. Larger α trusts which sensor more?
    The gyro.
  2. When is the accelerometer unreliable?
    When there is motion acceleration/vibration.
